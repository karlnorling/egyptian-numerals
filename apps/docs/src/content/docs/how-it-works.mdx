Egyptian hieroglyphs wrote numbers with one sign for each power of ten. A number repeats each sign as often as it needs, so there is no zero digit and no place value: the signs themselves say what they are worth.

## Repeating signs

A digit becomes that many copies of its sign, stacked in up to three rows. This is how one to nine lotuses look:

<DigitChart place={3} />

Groups run largest first. A column at Karnak gives 4,622 as four lotuses, six coils of rope, two hobbles and two strokes:

<div class="row">
  <Glyph value={4622} caption="4622" />
  <Glyph value={1993} caption="1993" />
  <Glyph value={1001} caption="1001" />
</div>

Empty powers are left out, which is why 1001 is a lotus and a stroke. Hieroglyphs stop at Heh, so this library stops at 9,999,999.

## Fractions

Scribes wrote fractions as sums of different unit fractions, 1/n, with the mouth sign _r_, "part", over the n. Two thirds had a sign of its own.

This library tries two ways to split the decimal part: taking 2/3 if it fits and then the largest unit fraction that fits, again and again, or splitting it over divisors of a power of ten. It keeps whichever needs fewer parts, then whichever has smaller denominators:

<Aside>
  A fraction that needs more than ten parts, or a part smaller than 1/9,999,999, is refused. Round
  it to fewer decimal places first.
</Aside>

## Zero and negatives

Egyptians had no zero digit. Accounts marked a balance of nothing with _nfr_, "complete" or "good", and this library draws nfr for 0. Negative numbers start with a bar, which is a modern addition.
